# Boutique Programming And The Dream Of Ideal Software

I 
It's always the same story with software engineers. The PM (Product Manager) comes up with an idea, and then takes that idea to their favored Computer Person. The Computer Person is a venerable wizard, sitting in a tall tower, wisely pondering the art of abstract symbol manipulation. They silently hear out the idea in its entirety, then ask some very specific questions, that seem strange and alien to the PM: "how many miliseconds can user wait for the answer of the proposed system", or "where will the user enter their password". The PM will answer these questions to the best of their ability, and then the wizard will furrow their brow and take an undisturbed minute to Think. Then they will dictate their iron verdict: which ambitions need to be scaled down, and what parts of the idea can be implemented. It is painful for the PM to give up some of the perfection of their platonic idea (footnote: in the industry this is called "killing your babies"), and it is painful for the programmer to build the parts of the system that don't fit neatly in the perfect platonic world of Code. 

II
Ask a programmer what their favorite programming language is. No, not the one that they *use* the most, but the one in their heart. You might hear about Haskell, and its mathematical perfection, you might hear about Rust, with its mechanical precision, and you might even hear about the Lisp, the language that God surely spoke, the old testament God, that spawned the world from His word, the word that can impose its will on nature as surely as it can impose its will on words themselves.

To a programmer, the idea of a programming language isn't about if-statements and for-loops, it's about the possibilities that open up when you can impress your intention so well that it's as if you're writing prose and not code at all. The study of turing machines tells us that computers can do near goddamn anything. How many sticks of RAM you have or the speed of your CPU basically doesn't matter, it's more than enough to solve most of humanity's issues, if only you figured out what code to write into the text box. The venerable wizard ponders this in their tower, but they don't tell the PM about it. 

III
Why don't you see more wise wizards working on beautiful recursive programming that opens up new possibilities? In short, it's because Elegant Software is a phase that all wizards go through, before they encounter the realities of our world. You might see a new programmer writing a text-editing application where every paragraph is an abstract Node that has abstract Nodes for the individual words and then the individual letters in words, and that could, in theory, be extended to display and edit just about anything. Hopefully they're not making this for work, because as any wizard has to learn eventually, elegance does not make for good software. In the real world, the more clever your code-architecture is, the quicker you yourself get tangled in it, and then your code becomes too convoluted to even look at.

That's not to say that smart architecture is a bad idea. In fact, good software architecture will multiply all your efforts, because it describes the "rules of the game" for your code, and it can be the difference between having to spend hours just to add a button, and doing it in 10 minutes and automatically getting warned you if you did something wrong. 

IV
I've techncally been programming computers for a decade, and for most of that decade, the process was similar: you get a task that takes 10 hours, spend the first 3 hours coming up with a good architecture to express the kind of intention that the task requires, then spend the other 7 hours writing code in this architecture, then spend another 5 cursing yourself for not spending a few more hours on architecture. 

This pattern changed for me in 2025, because of LLMs, because of course it did.

Now, you spend an hour on architecture, then describe what exactly you want your code to do, and then the most conscientious worker of all time writes the code inside of your architecture that does useful action, and then you're done. In fact, it's even more of a change than you might think: before, a big codebase might take a whole team with a backend programmer, frontend programmer, server programmer, manager for all the programmers, tester, UI designer, the list goes on and on. And the whole team needs to not step on each others' heels and walk in lockstep to hold up the weight of a giant codebase. These days, that has been replaced by a single person working with an AI, and a product manager telling them what to do. The blueprint for the entire system is stored in the brain of the programmer, and they act as a sort of intermediary, strategizing about how to implement the PM's vision, and how to not drown in complexity while doing so. The execution phase itself is like the slash of a skilled samurai: the blade accomplishes the intention of its owner in one single moment of perfect precision.

V
Think about what happened in 2025. The whole implementation team got compressed into the head of one programmer, and they can write as much code as they want instantly. The entire art of software is boiled down to its purest form: to make good decisions about how to compose the structure of symbols and concepts, to achieve maximum result with minimum complexity. The thing that decides the scope of what your code can do is the architecture, and you get incredible returns on investments if you know what decisions to make. This is what people mean by "taste" in softare engineers. 

So why then, do programmers still act like wizards in their high towers, telling the poor PM about which features are "realistic", and which features are "non-essential" for the product? You can build anything! With the right architecture, you could add features faster than the product manager can come up with new features to add, if only you could build the architecture where you don't lose track of what each part does.

This, I think, is the new big project in the field of software. No one yet understands how to build software with AI, and everyone wants to come up with a new methodology that would let them build reliable software quickly. There's been some ideas:
- use Rust to build exactly correct code
- throw huge amounts of spaghetti at the wall and discard entire chunks on a whim
- React frontend with a REST API and SQL database (this one is the classic, but we're already seeing it evolve)
- Complete spaghetti mayhem in self-contained boxes that interact with eachother through exact APIs
- Write code in English and use LLMs like a compiler. (this one is interesting)

The field of computer programming is nowhere near its maturity. Manufacturing, bureaucracy, military,logistics, governance, finance, and so many other fields are barely using software, and the software they do use is primitive and almost always has to interact with other systems through human intermediaries.

This post isn't even really about AI. Even without it, there's lots of new software innovation that has barely penetrated the world:
- prediction markets give software a way to react to new information and form robust beliefs
- blockchain lets you create systems that run on money rather than power-based sovereignty 
- manufacturing is no longer done by hands, and the machinery can manipulate matter without human hands or brains being involved, if it's programmed well
- everywhere with access to the sky can get electricity and internet

The only thing stopping us from running a lot more software is that we don't know how to build sprawling code systems. You can make progress on this problem if you have a shitty laptop, and a $20 subscription to the AI provider of your choosing. So much is possible. So go, cast spells into this world, and ignite it with new magic.
